A few days ago I watched TomorrowDevs' new video, [*Lavoro tech in Italia: l'AI ha scoperchiato il vaso di Pandora*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5h4nyIWUzDI) ("Tech jobs in Italy: AI opened Pandora's box", in Italian). It's a snapshot of the tech job market in October 2026 from someone who talks every day with every kind of profile, from entry-level developers to CTOs. It's worth watching in full. Here I collect the points that struck me most and explain why they hit close to home.

## Pandora's box

The core image of the video is this: AI didn't create our industry's problems, it brought them to the surface. Many teams adopted it on a massive scale, to the point of letting it write almost all the code, and that changed team dynamics overnight.

The video asks an uncomfortable question: if we loved writing code so much, why did we hand it off so quickly? The suggested answer isn't that we didn't like it. It's that many people are **tired**: yet another technology to learn in a rush, often at companies that value neither people nor skills. For some, AI was a relief. For others, it was the last straw.

## Uncertainty has moved to roles

The part that struck me most is the comparison with the dot-com era of the early 2000s. Back then, the uncertainty was about products: which idea would survive? Today it's the opposite. With AI, products get built in a day. The uncertainty has moved to **roles**.

And it isn't only about people starting out. Seniors, staff engineers and product managers are asking whether their skills still match the market: those who spent years on a niche technology, and those who did a bit of everything at a consultancy or a small company and can't tell how they compare to the "same" role elsewhere.

## Juniors pay the highest price

For juniors the picture is the toughest, and it matches what I see too:

- **Demand has dropped.** There are fewer junior openings than before.
- **The bar has gone up.** Where juniors do get hired, they're expected to already be used to complex work environments.
- **The paradox is still there.** "Junior with experience": but how do you get experience if nobody lets you start?
- **You're expected to know the whole lifecycle.** Work is shifting toward product: understanding how software is born, shipped and run in production, not just one piece of code.

The most important point, though, is another one. For years, a junior's growth relied on an unspoken deal with seniors: "you take these simpler tasks, and you'll learn the ropes". Today, seniors hand those boring, repetitive tasks to an AI agent. It's faster and needs no explaining.

The result is that **the gym where juniors used to grow is disappearing**. Not out of malice, but out of efficiency. And on your own, you often lack the tools to train elsewhere: nobody shows you how to put a project into production, how to write tests that hold up, how to read a pull request.

## Knowing how to write code is no longer enough

If AI generates the code, a developer's value shifts to everything around it:

- knowing **where** that code belongs and why;
- understanding how to **test** it and how to notice you broke something;
- getting it **into production** with a pipeline, not by hand;
- **reading** other people's code (AI's included) and giving and receiving reviews;
- noticing what happens **after** the release: logs, errors, metrics.

That's exactly what you learn on the job, next to someone more experienced. And it's exactly what's hardest to learn today.

## What you can actually do

If you're a junior or looking for your first job, some practical advice:

1. **Build a real project, end to end.** Not a tutorial clone: something with a frontend, a backend, a database, tests and a public URL.
2. **Use AI as a colleague, not an autopilot.** If you can't explain the code it wrote, it isn't yours yet.
3. **Work like you're on a team.** Branches, pull requests, readable commits. Whoever looks at your GitHub wants to see *how* you work.
4. **Get a human review.** Feedback from someone more experienced is what agents are taking away from juniors. You need to get it back.
